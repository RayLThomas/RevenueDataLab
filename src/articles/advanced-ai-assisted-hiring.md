All too often, a company needs a position filled and makes a bad decision on what role, duties, and skills to hire for. Much of this can be avoided and accomplished by one person leveraging AI instead of relying entirely on the usual process: talk to the future manager, talk to the team, ask what they need, and pull the most recent and relevant JD from the HR rep, etc.

This method is based upon two silent assumptions that need to be correct:

1. The manager knows what success actually looks like day by day, hour by hour.
2. The job description is accurate and reflects the current needs of the business.

Therein lies our problem statement and objective. We must solve for how to identify the correct skills, experiences, and traits that align with an accurate and complete job description. If we get the job description and role expectations correct, we have a much better chance of making a successful hire.

## Identifying What We Actually Need

How can we identify what we need? We can leverage AI to review the various sources and artifacts related to the role, things like:

- Prior performance reviews for former employees in similar roles.
- Transcripts of key meetings, either client-facing or internal, where the person in that role had a key responsibility.
- Salary bands and internal job requirement documents.
- SOPs related to the general company and especially the regular functions of the specific job.
- Top candidate resumes from the prior hiring cycle.
- Prior interview recordings, calendars, internal messaging, Slack chats, emails, etc., to review what the previous employee actually did and what role they played with customers and internal teams.
- Company knowledge bases, Notion, Google Drive, and similar platforms to see what deliverables were created by the last person in that role, which were most used, and by whom.
- Project management systems and support tickets to identify the types of problems this person was regularly responsible for solving.

AI can build a profile of the role based on this information and compare it against the existing job description. In doing so, we may uncover responsibilities that were never documented, skills that are listed as requirements but rarely used, or dependencies between departments that the hiring manager wasn't fully aware of.

We also need to account for any changes in the business since the last person was hired. Perhaps 30% of what that person did can now be automated, or perhaps another department has taken ownership of some of those responsibilities. This could materially change what we're looking for in a candidate and potentially even the salary band or level of seniority required.

I'd also use this opportunity to identify what we expect this person to accomplish within their first 90 days, six months, and year. Having those expectations defined before we begin interviewing gives us something to reference throughout the hiring process.

## Integrating This With Your ATS

Now you can combine this information with your Applicant Tracking System (ATS) to highlight candidates who have demonstrated competencies relevant to the actual needs of the business.

For example, you may have a requirement of five years of CRM administration experience. However, after reviewing the artifacts associated with the role, you find that much of what this person will be doing involves gathering business requirements, designing solutions, and working with multiple departments to implement them.

How much weight should we actually be giving those five years of CRM administration experience when evaluating applicants? Someone with two years of CRM experience and three years of business analysis experience might be a better fit for what we've identified.

We can use the findings from our initial research to establish and weight these competencies and have AI assist in reviewing resumes against them.

## AI-Assisted Interview Preparation

But don't stop here. You can actually make a skill that does interview prep for you, which will prepare the interviewer for the specific interviewee to uncover and push on self-reported competencies.

The skill can reference the candidate's resume, the job description, the role profile we created earlier, and even information about the interviewer and their area of expertise.

For example, if a candidate claims to have extensive experience in business process automation, we can pull an actual business problem from one of the artifacts we reviewed earlier and have the interviewer ask how they would go about solving it.

We can also identify which competencies we have little or no evidence of from their resume and make sure we allocate some portion of the interview to exploring those. The underlying evaluation criteria should remain consistent across candidates, but we have quite a bit of flexibility in how we go about assessing them.

If you're going to move that candidate on, you can use the transcripts to prepare the next interviewer as well. Have AI identify which competencies were sufficiently addressed, which need further validation, and which weren't discussed at all.

This should be particularly useful for companies that have three or four rounds of interviews involving people from different departments. Each interviewer can go into the interview knowing what has already been established and where their time and expertise would be most beneficial.

Rinse and repeat as candidates move through this process or are eliminated.

## Improving the Process With Each Hire

I'd take this a step further and incorporate the performance reviews of the new hire back into the process.

At six months or a year, we should have enough information to begin evaluating how accurately we identified the needs of the business and how well our interview process assessed the candidate's ability to fulfill those needs.

Perhaps the person we hired excelled in an area that we gave very little weight to during the interview process. Perhaps one of the competencies we considered critical to the role turned out to be relatively insignificant. We may even find that a particular interview question did a particularly poor job of assessing the competency it was intended to assess.

We can use these findings to update the original skill, more accurately define the job description, adjust the weighting of competencies on the ATS side, and improve the questions and scenarios used during interviews.

There are, of course, some considerations around data governance here. Performance reviews, employee communications, and candidate information need to be handled appropriately. We also need to be careful about introducing historical biases into our evaluation criteria and ensure that the people responsible for hiring are validating the findings and making the decisions.

What I find particularly interesting about this approach is that most companies already have much of the data necessary to accomplish it. They have meeting transcripts, performance reviews, documentation, emails, project management data, and an ATS. The information exists across a number of different systems, and until recently, it would have taken a considerable amount of someone's time to review and make use of all of it.

If we can integrate these systems and establish a repeatable process for reviewing this information, we can improve our understanding of what makes someone successful in a given role every time we hire for it. Over the course of several hiring cycles, I'd expect that to have a meaningful impact on the quality of our hires, the time it takes to get them up to speed, and potentially even our ability to retain them.
